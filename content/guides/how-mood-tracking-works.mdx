Mood tracking is simple in concept — log how you're feeling, regularly, and look back over time —
but it's easy to do in a way that produces noise instead of insight. This guide covers what mood
tracking is actually measuring, why single-day entries are nearly meaningless on their own, and
how to use the data once you have it.

## What a single log entry actually captures

A single mood log entry is a snapshot, not a verdict — it captures how you felt at one specific
moment, shaped by everything happening that day: how you slept, what you ate, who you talked to,
how a deadline went. It is not a measurement of your overall mental health, and treating any one
entry as a big signal ("I logged a bad day, something must be wrong") usually leads to
overreacting to normal variation.

The value of mood tracking isn't in any individual entry. It's in the shape of many entries laid
next to each other.

## Why patterns matter more than points

A single low mood day is just a low mood day — everyone has them. A low mood that shows up every
Sunday night, or every time a particular class meets, or consistently a day or two before your
period, is a pattern. Patterns are what mood tracking is actually good at surfacing, because they
require comparing many data points across time, which is exactly what human memory is bad at and
a logged history is good at.

This is also why consistency matters more than precision. A rough, two-tap mood log done daily
for a month tells you more than one extremely detailed entry done once.

## What to log alongside mood

Mood alone — just a number or a label — is useful, but mood plus a little context is far more
useful. A few fields worth pairing with a mood log:

- **A one-line note on what happened.** This is what turns "I felt anxious on Tuesday" into
  "I felt anxious on Tuesday, right before my EEE 209 CAT."
- **Sleep, roughly.** Sleep is one of the strongest predictors of next-day mood, and most people
  underestimate how much it's driving how they feel. See
  [sleep and mental health](/guides/sleep-and-mental-health).
- **Energy level, separate from mood.** It's possible to feel emotionally fine but physically
  drained, or vice versa — tracking them separately avoids conflating the two.

## Reading your own data honestly

When you look back over a few weeks of mood data, resist two common traps:

1. **Over-explaining a single bad day.** Not every dip needs a tidy explanation. Sometimes a
   day is just a day.
2. **Ignoring the repeat pattern because it's inconvenient.** If your mood reliably dips every
   time you skip a meal, or every Sunday before a heavy week, that's worth acting on even if the
   fix (eating more consistently, restructuring your week) is mildly annoying to implement.

A useful question to ask periodically: "What's the one pattern in here that, if I actually
addressed it, would change the most?"

## How often to log

Daily is a reasonable default, but the honest answer is: whatever frequency you'll actually
sustain. A mood log done three times a week, consistently, for two months is more useful than a
daily log abandoned after five days. Start with a frequency you're confident you'll keep up, and
increase it later if it's working.

## From tracking to action

Mood tracking is most useful when it feeds back into something you actually do differently — not
as a passive diary you never revisit. Weekly reflection (see
[benefits of reflection](/guides/benefits-of-reflection)) is the natural next step: a short,
regular look back at what the data is actually telling you, turned into one small adjustment for
the week ahead.
