## Summary

Mood tracking, as a structured form of self-monitoring, draws on a research tradition closely
tied to cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), where self-monitoring is a long-established
therapeutic technique. This page summarizes what that research generally shows.

## Self-monitoring as a clinical tool

Self-monitoring — having a person systematically record a target behavior, thought, or feeling —
has been used in CBT and related approaches for decades, both as an assessment tool (helping a
clinician understand a pattern) and as an intervention in its own right. There's a documented
"reactivity" effect in self-monitoring research: the act of tracking something can itself change
it, often in a positive direction, simply because attention to a pattern tends to increase
intentional control over it.

## What mood tracking specifically shows

Research on mood tracking, including studies using smartphone-based ecological momentary
assessment (logging mood multiple times a day in real-world settings, rather than relying on
retrospective recall), has found that people are often inaccurate when asked to recall how they
felt over a past period — recall tends to be biased toward how they feel right now, or toward a
few standout moments, rather than reflecting the actual average. Frequent, in-the-moment logging
avoids this recall bias and tends to produce a more accurate picture of someone's actual mood
patterns over time.

## Where the benefit seems to come from

The research generally points to pattern recognition, not the act of logging itself, as the main
source of benefit. A mood log that's never reviewed has limited value; a mood log that's
periodically looked back on — surfacing, for instance, a consistent dip on a particular day of
the week, or after a particular kind of interaction — gives people concrete, specific information
they can act on, rather than a vague sense that "lately has been hard."

## Limitations worth noting

Self-monitoring research also documents some downsides worth being aware of: tracking can become
a source of anxiety itself for some people (over-monitoring, or treating every dip as alarming),
and the quality of insight depends heavily on what's tracked alongside mood — mood data with no
context (sleep, events, energy) is harder to interpret than mood data paired with a few
contextual notes.

## Practical takeaways

- Logging mood in the moment, or close to it, tends to be more accurate than reconstructing how a
  whole day felt at the end of it.
- The real value shows up on review, not on entry — periodic look-backs are where patterns become
  visible.
- Pairing mood with a small amount of context (what happened, how you slept) makes the data far
  more useful than mood numbers alone.
